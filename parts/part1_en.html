Chapter 1: Clinical Knowledge Modelling

Welcome to the trailhead! Before we open any modeling tools or wrestle with specifications, we need to answer a deceptively simple question: Why bother modeling clinical data at all? In this chapter, we have a general look at the journey of health information across healthcare systems and understand the need for data models. We examine the distinct role of the Clinical Knowledge Modeller as the bridge between clinical practice and technical systems, and provide an initial overview of openEHR's multi-tier architecture.

1.1 Why model clinical data?

To understand openEHR, we first have to take a step back and look at the fundamental problem in healthcare informatics: What happens to clinical information once it is generated?

Imagine a routine scenario: a patient visits an outpatient clinic, has blood drawn, and the laboratory processes a test result.

From that moment on, the data embarks on a critical journey:

  1. Generation: The data is produced (e.g., a lab result, a blood pressure reading, or a pathology report).
  2. Persistence: The data is stored in an electronic system.
  3. Transmission: It is transferred across departments, hospitals, or primary care networks.
  4. Interpretation: A clinician in a completely different setting reads and interprets the data.
  5. Action: The clinician makes a treatment decision based on that interpretation.

This journey is depicted in simplified form in Figure 1.1. Information from a blood lab test is generated, stored, and sent to a different healthcare setting. There, the lab data is retrieved and shown to the healthcare professional. Based on this information, the clinician determines the patient's treatment.

It is of vital importance that during this process, no clinical data is altered or lost in translation.

Figure 1.1: The clinical data lifecycle from generation to clinical action.

When every hospital, department, or software vendor invents their own data formats and database tables, the translation of clinical data from one to another impacts the data quality. A field meant for "systolic blood pressure" in System A might get shoehorned into a generic "observation value" field in System B, stripping away important context like patient position, cuff size, or exertion.

To prevent loss of meaning of our health data, we need agreed-upon data models. If everyone uses the same model, no translation is necessary between systems and a total interoperability is achieved.

For a data model to succeed in real-world healthcare, it should at least:

  • Use Reusable Structures: Avoid reinventing the wheel. A single, comprehensive model for a "Blood Pressure" observation should be reusable in primary care, intensive care, research, and home monitoring.
  • Be Vendor-Neutral: The model must not belong to or depend on any single software vendor or proprietary database. Any health application developer should be able to build on top of it.
  • Be Future-Proof & Adaptable: Medical care evolves, guidelines change, and technology advances. The model must be flexible enough to accommodate new clinical requirements without breaking existing systems or invalidating historical data.

1.2 What is the role of a Clinical Knowledge Modeller in this?

If clinical data models are so important to the generation, persistence, and transport of clinical data, who is actually making those models? This is where the Clinical Knowledge Modeller enters the stage.

Clinicians speak medicine, while software developers speak databases, code, and APIs. When developers attempt to model medical concepts without clinical guidance, critical nuances get lost in translation. Conversely, when clinicians try to design software, technical feasibility and scalability often suffer.

The Clinical Knowledge Modeller acts as the vital bridge between these two worlds. Rather than writing software code, modellers work with domain experts to analyze clinical guidelines, workflows, and paper forms, translating them into formal, computable, and reusable clinical models. In short: you ensure that digital systems capture medical reality accurately without compromising patient safety or semantic meaning.

1.3 Why openEHR might be the right candidate for Clinical Knowledge Modelling

As noted earlier, preventing health data from being altered or degraded across healthcare settings requires common, agreed-upon specifications for clinical data models. One leading example of such a specification is openEHR.

Being open source, openEHR provides vendor-neutral, openly accessible specifications for managing, storing, querying, and exchanging Electronic Health Record (EHR) data. Thats what openEHR stands for: open specifications for EHRs.

This makes it possible to provide interoperable data models that can be shared and improved in a community of different developers.

The basic openEHR architecture

openEHR employs a multi-tier architecture that cleanly separates technical specifications from clinical knowledge. This structure comprises a technical Reference Model (RM), archetypes and templates at the clinical knowledge layer, and—at the data instance level—compositions. As illustrated in Figure 1.2, these layers build upon one another. In the following sections, we examine each of these fundamental levels.

Figure 1.2: The openEHR multi-tier architecture separating technical models from clinical concepts.

The Reference Model (RM)

At the base of openEHR are the foundational definitions of how data can be saved and which functions should be provided in applications to transform or receive information about the data. This is the Reference Model, which is essential for software engineers building applications, repositories, and services. It is disconnected from the clinical context because the software code does not need to change every time a new clinical protocol or guideline emerges.

You can find the technical reference model of openEHR here: https://specifications.openehr.org/

The clinical components of openEHR modelling

Separated from the technical layer (the Reference Model), Clinical Knowledge Modellers work in the clinical knowledge layer.

In the following paragraphs, we will have a short overview of the critical components of archetypes. templates and compositions. Don't worry, we will go further in depth in subsequent chapters.

Archetypes

An archetype is a universal, granular, and reusable building block that represents a discrete clinical concept (e.g., Blood Pressure, Weight, Height). To build one, we use data types defined in the Reference Model to structure the individual clinical data elements that make up the concept.

Templates

An openEHR template represents a specific clinical context, tailored by the Clinical Knowledge Modeller for a concrete workflow, document, or form (e.g., Discharge Summary, Emergency Triage, or Oncology Care Pathway). They consist in a structure of archetypes.

While archetypes provide the universal clinical concepts, templates are what define how clinical data is actually validated and persisted in an openEHR system.

For a Clinical Knowledge Modeller, the template is the ultimate deliverable (even though a lot of work goes into making the perfect archetypes!).

Compositions

When clinicians record data in a clinical application, the application they use generates "compositions" that strictly conform to the rules defined in a template and the Reference Model. These documents are what actually store the patient's data in the Clinical Data Repository (CDR).

In theory, compositions fall outside the day-to-day scope of a Clinical Knowledge Modeller. In the end it is the software engineer that is responsible for the creation of compositions based on the Clinical Knowledge Modellers templates.

However, as we will explore later, modellers still need to keep a close eye on this creation of compositions to ensure that what gets persisted truly reflects the intended clinical model.

Chapter 2: "Building blocks" of openEHR

With the overarching architecture established, we can now examine the structural components that make up openEHR. In this chapter, we analyze the core building blocks from the ground up, starting with the Data Value (DV_*) classes that capture precise clinical nuances, through to the design and classification of reusable archetypes, examine how archetypes are contextualized into templates, and walk through the role of versioned compositions and contributions.

2.1 Fundamental building blocks of openEHR

So now that we had a look at the basic architecture of openEHR and have a basic understanding of the most important components, we will go further into detail.

Data Value Classes

Before diving into the details of archetypes, we need to understand how individual data elements are represented in openEHR. As one might expect, a clinical measurement, a date, and a clinician's free-text comment cannot be treated or stored in the same way.

In openEHR, clinical elements are represented using Data Value classes (all prefixed with DV_). Crucially, these are not just primitive data types like basic integers, reals, characters, or strings found in programming languages. Instead, they are structures designed to contain as much information as possible about a data element.

openEHR defines a comprehensive set of Data Value classes that we can categorize into key areas:

  • Text and Coded Text (free text, terminology-bound values)
  • Quantities and Measurements (numbers with units, proportions, scales)
  • Date and Time (dates, times, durations, and timestamps)
  • Identifiers and References (URIs, object identifiers)
  • Encapsulated Data (multimedia, parsable formats)
  • Boolean Values and States (flags, state-machine values)
  • Intervals (ranges across quantities, dates, or counts)

Figure 2.1 provides an overview of the core Data Value types available in openEHR. You can find the specificcations of the data value classes here: Data Value Classes.

Figure 2.1: Overview of openEHR Data Value types.

As a Clinical Knowledge Modeller, one of your primary responsibilities is deciding how each clinical data element should be represented. When modelling a data element, you need to choose the appropriate data value type from openEHR's Reference Model (RM).

Let's look at an example to see how this works in practice by observing the DV_QUANTITY class. To read the specifications, depending on your preferences, several resources are available:

  • Official openEHR Data Types Specification: The formal, authoritative definition of the Reference Model.
  • Interactive openEHR RM Browser: A searchable, browsable tool for exploring RM classes, hierarchies, and properties easily.
  • NeoEHR UML Diagrams: A visual UML representation showing the inheritance tree and structural relationships for DV_QUANTITY.

Figure 2.2 demonstrates how different resources provide distinct perspectives on the openEHR specifications.

Figure 2.2: Reference Model structure for DV_QUANTITY.

At first glance, a quantitative clinical measurement seems straightforward: just a number paired with a unit. For example:

  • Weight: 82 kg
  • Blood Pressure: 120 mmHg
  • Body Temperature: 36.7 °C

If we have a look at the Reference Model that defines the DV_QUANTITY, we can see that this is true: there is obligatory a magnitude attribute that stores a real number and a units attribute to store a string.

But the Reference Model also allows for optional attributes:

  • normal_range: Defines the expected normal interval for the quantity (e.g. standard physiological laboratory reference limits).
  • other_reference_ranges: Stores additional tagged reference ranges beyond the normal range, such as critical, therapeutic, or toxic thresholds.
  • precision: Specifies the number of decimal places to preserve (a precision of 0 indicates an integer, 1 indicates one decimal place, while -1 means arbitrary/floating precision).
  • units_display_name: A localized or user-friendly display label for the unit string (e.g. "mmHg" or "beats/min") to tailor presentation in clinical interfaces.
  • units_system: Identifies the terminology or unit system used to define the unit (typically Unified Code for Units of Measure / UCUM).
  • accuracy (inherited from DV_AMOUNT): Quantitative measurement error or tolerance limit expressed either in units or as a percentage.
  • accuracy_is_percent (inherited from DV_AMOUNT): A boolean flag indicating whether the value in accuracy is a percentage (true) or given in the physical units of the quantity (false).
  • magnitude_status (inherited from DV_QUANTIFIED): Indicates whether the magnitude is exact or an approximation (e.g. =, <, >, ~=).
  • normal_status (inherited from DV_ORDERED): A coded term indicating where the value falls relative to the normal range (e.g. normal, low, high, critically high).
As you can see, a lot of care has been put into making the class as versatile as possible with a lot of attributes.

Archetypes

Now that we know about data value types, we can imagine that we can use those to represent a clinical concept.

Take blood pressure for example. We can create a simple archetype by joining two DV_QUANTITY, one for diastolic pressure and another for systolic pressure. Hmm... maybe we also should add a DV_TEXT, so we can store a comment. Maybe some element for the device used... and the condition of the patient... and so on.

You can see that the clinical concept of "blood pressure" becomes quite complex, as we want to keep it as broad as possible to fit into different clinical contexts. That's why we say that archetypes are maximal. Later, when putting them into context (i.e. a template), we can constrain anything that does not apply (and is modeled as optional in the archetype). This makes archetypes reusable, which is one of the main goals of openEHR.

In Figure 2.4 you can see a representation of an archetype for the measurement of blood pressure. Can you see how many clinical parameters are included? I think we can agree that there is a lot more than anticipated.

Figure 2.4: A representation of the blood pressure archetype in the international CKM. View blood pressure archetype in international CKM

Anatomy of an Archetype ID

With potentially thousands of archetypes shared across international repositories, how do systems tell them apart without confusion?

They rely on the Archetype ID. Instead of giving models arbitrary names, openEHR uses a structured identifier that should reveal the archetype's purpose, class, and version at a glance.

Every archetype follows this standardized convention. Let's see an example:

openEHR-EHR-OBSERVATION.blood_pressure.v2
  • openEHR → Standard / issuer
  • EHR → Reference Model package (clinical record)
  • OBSERVATION → Archetype Class
  • blood_pressure → Clinical concept name
  • v2 → Major version

Behind the Scenes: The ADL Format and Multilingual Design

Multilingual archetypes

Apart from containing data element that provide a place to store health data, archetypes contain the clinical definition of every element to ensure that the archetype is used correctly.

Archetypes are multilingual in nature, which means they carry official translations within the model itself. This is great news for Clinical Knowledge Modellers, as we often have several official languages that we might want to model in.

At the same time, it means that only archetypes sharing the same language can be combined to create templates. So it might be necessary to add a translation to an archetype before being able to use it in your local context. It is recommended to start archetypes in English, because they can then be added to the international CKM.

Can I read an archetype file?

Archetypes are written in a human-readable format called ADL (Archetype Definition Language). This means we can open the .adl file and read the content directly. It is a bit complex though, as ADL contains several distinct sections and syntaxes. Luckily, we as Clinical Knowledge Modellers do not have to hand-code these files thanks to modern visual tools.

You can find more information on the structure of an archetype here: https://specifications.openehr.org/releases/AM/latest/Overview.html#_archetype_definition_language_adl

Where do archetypes come from? - A short word about the international CKM

Before you even think about creating an archetype from scratch: Don't reinvent the wheel. The International Clinical Knowledge Manager (CKM) is the global library and governance hub for openEHR archetypes.

Why participate in the CKM?
  • Clinical consensus: Archetypes in the CKM aren't made in isolation by a single engineer or hospital. They undergo rigorous, multi-round peer reviews by clinicians, terminologists, and modellers across the globe.
  • Global interoperability: When your hospital in Barcelona, a clinic in Norway, and a health tech startup in Australia all use the same CKM blood_pressure archetype, your data is instantly semantic-compatible across borders.
  • You have a voice: Medicine has regional nuances. By participating in CKM reviews, submitting translations, or providing feedback on draft archetypes, you ensure that global health data standards reflect real-world clinical practice in your region.

The golden rule for us Clinical Knowledge Modellers is to search the CKM first, adopt what exists, and contribute back.

Just to be clear: you can make your own models and use them locally without publishing them to the international CKM, but you would be missing out on the experience and review of a lot of clever experts!

Archetype flavors - the different classes

An archetype is not just a structure of data elements that represents a clinical concept. Archetypes have a place in the clinical workflow. A prescription is not the same as a medication administered, and therefore requires different attributes to be stored.

In general, we can divide archetypes in there groups:

Structural Classes

  • COMPOSITION - Document container.
  • SECTION - Content organizer for a COMPOSITION.
  • CLUSTER - Reusable group of data elements (can be thought of as an extension or modular branch).
Entry Classes
  • ACTION - For actions performed.
  • EVALUATION - For clinical opinions, diagnoses, or assessments.
  • INSTRUCTION - For future actions or orders.
  • OBSERVATION - For measured and reported data.
Admin Entry Class
  • ADMIN_ENTRY - For administrative data.

What is so "special" in the different archetype flavors?

The different archetype classes have special attributes that make them fit for their intended purpose. The attributes can be filled with content either during the editing of the archetype or once the archetype gets put into a template.

Be aware that we only look at the elements that can be added by the Clinical Knowledge Modeller. There is a lot more information that can be added in the final composition that is defined by the Reference Model.
Structural Archetypes
COMPOSITION — The Root Document Container
  • In Archetype In context/other_context, you can add administrative elements or define CLUSTER slots.
  • In Template Under content, you assemble the list of clinical entries and sections that form the document.
  • In Template Slot specific CLUSTER archetypes into any slots defined in other_context.
SECTION — Document Organizer
  • In Template Under items, nest entry archetypes or subordinate sections to create structured headings.
CLUSTER — Reusable Modular Elements
  • In Archetype Define data elements and nested cluster slots starting directly from the root level.
  • In Template Attach this cluster into an open slot in any Entry or Composition.
Clinical Entry Archetypes
OBSERVATION — Measurements & Scores
  • In Archetype data: Define events (point, interval, summary) and clinical elements. Subsequent events inherit elements from the primary event definition.
  • In Archetype protocol: Record measurement metadata (e.g. instrument calibration, patient position).
  • In Template Attach CLUSTER archetypes into slots defined in data or protocol.
EVALUATION — Diagnoses & Summaries
  • In Archetype data: Document opinions, clinical evaluations, or diagnostic assessments.
  • In Archetype protocol: Document criteria, guidelines, or scoring methods.
  • In Template Attach CLUSTER archetypes into slots defined in data or protocol.
INSTRUCTION — Orders & Plans
  • In Archetype activities: Configure the activity description (e.g. "Order") with clinical elements.
  • In Archetype protocol: Capture request identifiers and order tracking details.
  • In Template Set activity occurrence (e.g. 0..*) to allow multiple orders within one instruction, and attach CLUSTER archetypes into activity slots.
ACTION — Performed Activities
  • In Archetype description: Record details of the action performed.
  • In Archetype protocol: Store procedure metadata and linked Order IDs.
  • In Archetype ism_transition: Map activity steps to official openEHR state codes (e.g. 526::planned, 245::active, 531::completed).
  • In Template Attach CLUSTER archetypes to verify executed details (e.g. actual medication dose or pizza toppings baked).
Administrative Archetypes
ADMIN_ENTRY — Operational Metadata
  • In Archetype data: Record non-clinical operational data elements (e.g. bed management, admission/discharge details).

Cluster vs. Cluster vs. Cluster

We have mentioned the CLUSTER archetype before, and you might have encountered the term "cluster" in other contexts as well. In our own modeling work, this shared name has caused more than a few moments of confusion! It is very important to clarify which kind of cluster we are talking about. In openEHR, you encounter "clusters" in three distinct forms:

  1. As a CLUSTER archetype: A standalone, reusable archetype class (e.g., openEHR-EHR-CLUSTER.pizza_topping.v0).
  2. As an internal CLUSTER of data elements: A grouped container of elements modeled directly inside an existing archetype to organize related fields together.
  3. As a CLUSTER slot: An archetype slot (allow_archetype) defined inside an archetype specifically to plug in external cluster archetypes when assembling a template.

In Figure 2.5 you can see where to identify the different cluster in the Archetype Designer during developing archetypes.

Templates

So far, we have looked at Archetypes as reusable, universally agreed-upon clinical concepts. For really using them in a clinical application, we have to structure them into templates.

In a template, we do basically two things:

  1. Put archetypes into hierarchical structures.
  2. Constrain the maximal archetypes down to our application's exact needs.

The template - a structure of archetypes

For the creation of a template, different archetypes are inserted into each other, starting with a COMPOSITION archetype as base.

As shown in Figure 2.6, the template starts from a root COMPOSITION archetype, using SECTION archetypes to structure the document. Clinical Entry archetypes are then nested within these sections and extended using modular CLUSTER archetypes. As this tree-like hierarchy expands, its structure can quickly grow in complexity.

Figure 2.6: Example of a template structure made of different archetypes (based on View International Patient Summary in the international CKM).

Be aware that not all archetype flavors can be arbitrarily connected between themselves:

  • A COMPOSITION can contain CLUSTERs (if a slot has been modeled in other_context), ENTRYs, and SECTIONs.
  • A SECTION can contain a list of ENTRY and nested SECTION archetypes.
  • An ENTRY archetype can contain CLUSTERs if a slot has been modeled in the archetype.
  • A CLUSTER archetype can contain nested CLUSTERs if a slot has been modeled.

Template Design Strategy: Workflow Forms vs. Data Storage

When starting out, it is tempting to design a template exactly like a paper form: a single, all-in-one document that mirrors an entire clinical encounter from admission to discharge.

However, in openEHR, a template is not just a user interface form—it defines how data is permanently persisted, versioned, and retrieved. Modeling one giant template for an entire workflow causes two significant practical problems:

  1. Inefficient versioning and data duplication: In openEHR, any change in the data you want to send implies sending the entire composition with the whole data set. If a template contains triage vitals, doctor notes, and nursing care plans all in one, adding a single temperature reading requires the clinical system to resubmit and version the entire document, repeatedly cloning unchanged data.
  2. Complex, fragile queries (AQL): Every level of nesting you create in a template forms the data path used to query it later. Deep, convoluted hierarchies make writing Archetype Query Language (AQL) queries harder to maintain and slower to run.
Ask yourself: Who documents this data, and when? If two activities occur at different times or by different actors (for example, routine vital sign measurements vs. a discharge summary), model them as separate, modular templates. Design for the lifecycle of the data, not just the layout of a screen.

Constraining archetypes and data elements

As mentioned before, archetypes are modeled maximal, to be applicable to many clinical contexts. This measn that in a specific context, not all modeled data elements are needed. Therefore, in the template, archetypes have to be constrained.

The constraints applied to archetypes in a template are usually:

  • Remove unnecessary data elements: Hide or set occurrences to 0 for data points you do not collect in your specific workflow (e.g., removing cuff size or tilt angle from a standard outpatient blood pressure check).
  • Restrict value sets: Limit allowed units or choices (e.g., restricting temperature to only Cel, or narrowing a diagnostic dropdown to specific SNOMED CT codes relevant to your department).
  • Rename nodes: Customize screen labels to match local clinical vocabulary or department conventions without altering the underlying archetype identity.
  • Clone nodes: Duplicate a generic archetype slot or element to capture multiple structured occurrences (e.g., cloning a generic symptom section for "Primary Symptom" and "Secondary Symptom").
  • Make elements mandatory: Tighten cardinalities from optional (0..1) to required (1..1) to ensure critical information is never omitted.

With this, we create a template that represents a concrete clinical context and fulfills the specific requirements of a clinical application, use case, or form.

Technical Representations: OET vs. OPT (The Cupcake Analogy)

When working on templates, in practice we encounter two different file formats when working with openEHR:

  • openEHR Template (.oet): The authoring format created and saved by template design tools (like the openEHR Archetype Designer).
  • Operational Template (.opt): The compiled, self-contained artifact generated from the .oet and its underlying archetypes.

⚠️ Watch out!
The OET format is not canonical in openEHR, but it is crucial in practice:
  1. It is the only template format that can be imported into the CKM (Clinical Knowledge Manager).
  2. It is the only format that connects specific archetypes to the template.
Why this matters:
Imagine a "Vital Signs" template that contains the "Body Mass Index" archetype. An archetype is uniquely identified by different values: ID, UID, Revision and Hash. For example:
  • ID: openEHR-EHR-OBSERVATION.body_mass_index.v2
  • UID: df5e3989-6b48-45e0-b3a0-185983a705a1
  • Revision: 2.1.0
  • Integrity Hash (MD5-CAM-1.0.1): 45CDA8C2180DDA3CAB6EBB6AF603493D
Archetypes evolve, and different templates often rely on specific revisions. The OET file contains an integrity check at the end that identifies the specific revision by both the ID and hash.

The OPT file, in contrast, only identifies the archetype by its ID. This means that with only the OPT file in hand, it is impossible to reconnect or trace the exact revision of the archetype that was used to build the template.

One way to think about the assembly of archetypes into templates and the role of OET and OPT is baking a cupcake (the cupcake being our openEHR template).

The traditional "LEGO block" metaphor works well for explaining modularity in openEHR, but it falls short when explaining constraints: in the LEGO® world, modifying, cutting, or repainting bricks during assembly is taboo. In openEHR, however, assembling a template involves actively constraining those blocks to suit a specific clinical context. Figure 2.8 illustrates the cupcake analogy, which will be explained in the following sections.

Figure 2.8: The cupcake analogy of template formats.

The Ingredients: The Archetypes - Just like you have flour, sugar, eggs, and chocolate chips sitting in your kitchen pantry, archetypes are our standard, universal ingredients. Each archetype is a standalone, well-defined clinical concept in its purest, reusable form. On their own, they are not yet a complete meal or a specific clinical document. They are the modular building blocks waiting to be combined.

When combining our ingredients to create the cupcake, we need a recipe to follow. In the case of openEHR, this recipe is a file with instructions on which archetypes to include and which changes to make to them. When archetypes are integrated into a template, several things can be changed, for example, the names of variables, the occurrence of variables (variables can disappear!), and restrictions on allowed list elements.

The Recipe: The OET File - The recipe for creating the template is the OET file. It is the official instruction on how to build the template and can be edited during development. In this form, the template explicitly references the exact revision of each archetype used, along with the specific constraints applied to it. It is vital for the development process and its documentation. We have to realize that a template is more than the sum of its archetypes.

The Baked cupcake: The OPT File - With this recipe in hand, we can then create the template. Basically, we bake the cupcake, so its ingredients cannot be changed afterwards. The resulting OPT file is the final template that is uploaded to the Clinical Data Repository (CDR). It does not contain the original archetypes separately, but rather their final form with all the changes and constraints baked in. There is no possibility to know the exact revision of the archetypes used. It is the non-editable, runtime version of the template.

A Word About Multilingual Templates

As mentioned earlier, archetypes are multilingual by design, meaning they can contain translations for both the clinical concept and its individual data elements in addition to the primary language. When creating templates, we are only allowed to combine archetypes that share matching languages.

In theory, the template itself can store translations for every data element. In practice, however, issues arise when a template includes multiple instances of the same archetype. Experience shows that when compiling a multilingual OPT, only the first instance of an archetype retains its translations. As a result, translations for subsequent instances do not appear in the generated OPT file.

Another limitation is that the OET format does not explicitly specify translation languages. The CKM, which accepts only OET files for template uploads, renders templates exclusively in their primary language and allows downloading the OPT in only one language at a time.

For these reasons, authoring a single template that bundles multiple translations is currently not recommended. The most reliable workaround is to keep the template in its primary language and manage official translations in a separate external file that consuming applications can reference. How to standardize this could work an ongoing topic of discussion within the openEHR community. If we ever meet in person, definitely bring this up: I am very passionate about translations and especially the use of labels in data element paths (spoiler warning: definitely not a fan!).

Please keep in mind that many file format quirks stem from our reliance on ADL 1.4. While these limitations are well-known and addressed in newer specifications (such as ADL 2), today's production tools and clinical repositories remain predominantly anchored in the ADL 1.4 ecosystem.

Compositions

Once a clinical context has been defined in an Operational Template (OPT), it is uploaded to the Clinical Data Repository (CDR). The CDR is not only where we store the template, but also where the clinical data recorded from the patient is persisted.

When clinical data is entered into an application in a healthcare setting, it is packaged into a composition according to the rules defined in the template. Confusingly, there is also an archetype class called COMPOSITION that defines the root of this document.

A composition is the actual clinical document or record instance (e.g., a triage assessment, a laboratory report, or a discharge summary). Every composition is bound to and validated against a specific template, and can also contain data elements defined directly by the Reference Model.

As Clinical Knowledge Modellers, we are not directly involved in generating compositions in software—that is the responsibility of the software engineers building applications that consume openEHR templates to structure the patient's data. However, we sometimes need to guide engineers regarding data elements that are not defined explicitly in the template, but can be added because they exist in the Reference Model.

Compositions are typically represented in JSON. This is convenient because anyone can open the file and inspect its contents. While Clinical Knowledge Modellers should not get lost in code, understanding the structure of the data we model is essential. We will see exactly how this looks in Part III, where we construct a composition from scratch.

Contributions

When we say that we send the patient's clinical data in a composition document, we are simplifying the process a bit.

We have to communicate somehow to the CDR what this composition constitutes: is it a new set of data, is it a modification of existing data, or do we want to change the status of the document to "deprecated", for example?

For us to be able to transmit this to the CDR, we send the composition as a versioned document (so we know which version we want to create or modify) inside a contribution.

It is like putting the filled-out form with patient clinical data (the composition) into an envelope that specifies the version (forming the versioned composition), and then placing that inside another envelope (the contribution) before sending it out to the CDR. Figure 2.9 illustrates this "envelope in an envelope" analogy.

Figure 2.9: A composition is always identified as a version of a document and sent in a contribution.

The cool thing about contributions is that they can contain various compositions with different purposes. For example, you can send some compositions that are brand new and others that are modifications to previous ones, all in one go. That saves you from having to send the compositions one by one.

In many cases, the client libraries and API functions that enable us to post a composition to the CDR wrap the composition and send it inside a contribution automatically, without the sender needing to build the envelope manually.

Chapter 3: Storage and Retrieval of openEHR artefacts

Designing clinical models in theory is not all that is required to run those models. The models have to be deployed and managed. In this chapter, we look at the runtime environment where openEHR artefacts live and operate. We examine how the Clinical Data Repository (CDR) organises clinical records and introduce Archetype Query Language (AQL) for asking clinical data back.

3.1 The clinical knowledge modelling environment

In clinical knowledge modelling, we have to create, manage, and put openEHR templates into production. To do this, we use three specialized environments:

  1. An environment to create and edit archetypes and templates.
  2. An environment to manage, review, and publish the archetypes and templates (and related artefacts) created.
  3. An environment to store the templates and persist compositions based on those templates.

In practice, we use:

  • Editing / Authoring: The openEHR Archetype Designer made by Better, which is free and web-based.
  • Governance & Publication: A local Clinical Knowledge Manager (CKM).
  • Production & Storage: A Clinical Data Repository (CDR), in our case based on HIP EHRbase by vitagroup. For testing and learning, you can use the open EHRbase sandbox.

Figure 3.1 illustrates the clinical knowledge modelling environment. We use the Archetype Designer to author and edit archetypes, which are then managed in both a local and the international CKM. Using these curated archetypes from the CKM, we assemble templates in the Archetype Designer and manage them in the CKM as well, where the system tracks the exact connections between archetypes and templates. The template is then exported from the CKM and uploaded into the CDR. Once the template is published there, clinical applications can submit compositions (packaged inside contributions) to the repository. Finally, these stored compositions can be queried using the Archetype Query Language (AQL).

Figure 3.1: The typical ecosystem for openEHR Clinical Knowledge Modellers with the main fundamental building blocks.

A brief look into the CDR

The Clinical Data Repository (CDR) is at the heart of a clinical data workflow. Here we persist the clinical data and the templates. Also, the CDR governs access and handles the sending and receiving of data.

One important concept: When a clinical professional uses an application to see data of a patient, the data can come from different repositories, such as a document server, terminology server or the master patient index (see Figure 3.2).

It is important to us as Clinical Knowledge Modellers to be concious about what we model in openEHR. Only the clinical data has to be modelled for the openEHR CDR. Identifying data of the patient, for example, is not stored in the openEHR CDR, but are referenced from the Master Patient Index.

Figure 3.2: It is important not to model all information that the clinical professional wants to see in openEHR. Some information comes from several sources.

Organization in the CDR (EHR, Compositions, Templates, etc.)

When starting out with openEHR, it is sometimes difficult to know what the CDR does. Let's describe this in a simplified way.

Our Clinical Data Repository (CDR) in openEHR acts as a filing cabinet. Its main functions are to store data and facilitate data retrieval (see Figure 3.3).

Figure 3.3: The CDR as a file cabinet. An analogy that helps to understand the management of EHR, compositions and templates.

The data of a patient's clinical health record is stored in the CDR as an Electronic Health Record (EHR). One patient has one EHR that stores all clinical information over the lifespan of the patient. Think of the EHR as the patient's individual, lifelong master folder in the repository's digital filing cabinet. It is identified by a unique, pseudonymous identifier (ehr_id), keeping clinical data decoupled from direct demographic identifiers. All compositions and contributions created for that patient throughout their lifetime are stored inside this single master EHR container.

Apart from the medical records, the CDR stores templates. Every template is a rule set for the creation of a specific health record, such as the data recorded in a first visit to a GP, a laboratory result, or a Patient Reported Outcome. The template is never changed or "filled out". It lives in the CDR separate from patient data, serving only as a structural blueprint.

When a medical professional wants to record data for the patient, they create a document structured as determined by the rule set (the template). This document is called a composition and can be seen as being equal to a filled-out form. This form is then sent to the CDR to save the clinical record.

To do so, we specify its version. The version is saved because we can create, modify, or change the state of a composition. Every change creates a new version, ensuring that no historical data is ever overwritten or lost.

To send the versioned composition to the patient's health record, the composition is packed into an envelope called a contribution. It can hold one or several compositions, so one or more created or updated records can be committed to the patient's health record together as a single event.

The data stored in the composition can be queried via the Archetype Query Language (AQL). This can be done by creating ad hoc queries or by using predefined AQLs that are stored in the CDR. It is a good idea to have predefined, tested and validated AQLs that can be governed, instead of allowing anyone to execute any AQL in a CDR production environment.

3.2 AQL queries - ask for your data back

Once a composition has been send to the CDR, we have to ask, how we can access this data. One option is to recover the composition entirely, which can be done, but you would have to know the unique identifier of this composition and you cannot access a specific part or element of the composition.

If we don't know the identifier or want to search for clinical data in various compositions, we can use a special language, the Archetype Query Language (AQL). AQL is similar to SQL, that you might have worked with if you are familiar with relational databases.

Without diving into too much technical detail here (we are going into more detail in Part III), in broad strokes AQL (Archetype Query Language) gives us the power to retrieve specific clinical data from the CDR. For example:

Concerning a single patient:

  • Give me a list of all compositions stored for a specific patient.
  • Give me all blood pressure measurements for a patient, sorted in chronological order.
  • Give me a list of active allergies recorded for a patient.

Concerning a demographic population:

  • Give me all blood pressure measurements for patients who are male, over 50 years of age, and have a BMI greater than 26.2 kg/m².

While simple queries targeted at a single patient are fast, you can easily imagine that querying across a large population is computationally expensive. That is why we, as Clinical Knowledge Modellers, need to understand AQL: we must design our models thinking not only about how data is stored, but also about the strategies required to query and retrieve that data efficiently.
